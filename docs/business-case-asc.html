Business case · US ambulatory surgery centers

The center sends her home in four hours. Then nobody is watching.

Why Mend’s buyer is the ambulatory surgery center, and what it is worth to one.

Total joints moved to the ambulatory setting faster than the post-operative safety infrastructure did. A hospital that discharges a hip replacement on day three has a ward, a night nurse, an observation period and a crash team standing behind that decision. An ASC discharging the same operation at four hours has a phone number.

That asymmetry is the entire commercial argument. In the hospital, Mend duplicates a safety net that already exists — which is why it is a hard sell there and why the incumbent deterioration scores live in that market. In the ASC, Mend is the safety net. There is nothing else between discharge and the two-week wound check.

The shiftWhy the buyer exists now and didn’t five years ago

Medicare’s removal of total knee and then total hip arthroplasty from the inpatient-only list, and their subsequent addition to the ASC covered-procedures list, converted a hospital operation into an ambulatory one and moved the volume accordingly. Commercial payers followed, generally faster.

The clinical community solved the intraoperative and anaesthetic problems of same-day joints convincingly. What it did not solve is the fourteen days after the patient leaves. Venous thromboembolism does not peak on the day of surgery. Prosthetic joint infection declares itself in weeks two to four. Neither is detectable at the twenty-four-hour follow-up call, which is the only structured contact most centers make.

Ambulatory surgery centers took on the operation and the discharge, but inherited none of the surveillance that used to sit around them.

The economicsThree value lines, in the order they actually close

Dominant term

Case capacity

Centers decline otherwise-suitable patients over age, comorbidity, ASA class or living alone. A defensible monitored safety net lets the medical director widen selection criteria. Every recovered case carries full facility contribution margin.

Reliable

Nursing labour

Post-operative follow-up contact is already staffed and already paid for. Mend covers the whole risk window instead of one call at twenty-four hours, and returns most of those nurse hours to the schedule.

Real but modest

Avoided rescues

An unplanned hospital visit after an ambulatory joint is a quality-reported event, an uncompensated rescue, and a referral-pattern problem. Fewer of them is worth money — just less money than the capacity line.

Sequencing matters when you pitch this. Cost-savings arguments get routed to a committee. Capacity arguments get routed to the surgeon-owners, who are the people whose income rises when the schedule fills. Lead with capacity.

Where the model is fragile

Set the declined-patient count to zero and the default case turns negative — roughly a fourteen thousand dollar annual shortfall at ninety-five dollars per case. Labour and avoided rescues alone do not cover the price. The whole thing rests on the capacity term.

That is worth stating plainly rather than hiding, because it dictates what to do next: validate the capacity assumption with three medical directors before building anything else, and be prepared to price well below ninety-five dollars for a center that will not widen its criteria. A business case with one load-bearing assumption is fine. Not knowing which one it is, is not.

Deliberately excluded from the model

Remote monitoring reimbursement. The earlier framing of this product leaned on RPM and RTM billing as a revenue line. That is unsafe to assume in orthopaedics: hip and knee arthroplasty carry a 90-day global surgical period, and routine post-operative care inside that window is bundled into the surgical fee. Whether remote monitoring can be separately billed during the global period is contested and needs a coding opinion before it appears on any slide. The model above therefore clears on operating value alone — if the billing question resolves favourably, it is upside rather than the foundation.

Route to marketWhere to start and how it compounds

  1. Physician-owned orthopaedic ASCs running a total joints programmeHighest pain, shortest decision chain, and the owners are the surgeons whose schedule the capacity argument fills. One medical director can say yes.
  2. ASC management companies and MSOsThe large operators run centers in the hundreds under shared clinical governance. One contract propagates. This is where the economics stop being linear.
  3. Bundled-payment conveners and at-risk orthopaedic groupsOrganisations already carrying financial risk for the 90-day episode have the clearest reason to pay for what happens inside it.
  4. Hospital outpatient departments, then post-acuteSame clinical problem, slower procurement. Approach once the ASC evidence base exists, not before.

The last row is the structural opening. ASCs are fragmented across ASC-specific information systems rather than the large enterprise records, which is precisely why the incumbent hospital vendors have not reached them. A product built for the ASC first inherits a segment nobody is defending.

Mend’s own differentiation inside that opening is narrow and defensible: a voice call rather than an app, so an eighty-two-year-old is reachable; objective vitals fused with what she reports, so escalation does not rest on self-report alone; and a deterministic rule engine that produces an auditable reason for every verdict rather than a language model’s opinion.

EvidenceHow to make the capacity claim defensible

The capacity argument carries the model, and right now it rests on an assumption: that a center would genuinely widen its selection criteria given a monitored safety net. That is testable, and cheaply.

  • Ask three medical directors how many patients they declined last year on post-operative safety or social grounds, and what would change their mind. This is a conversation, not a study, and it either validates the dominant term or kills it.
  • Run the engine in shadow mode against a retrospective cohort where the outcome is already known. It is the only setting where ground truth exists, and it produces the sensitivity and specificity figures the clinical sale eventually requires.
  • Instrument the first deployment for the one metric that matters commercially: cases accepted that would previously have been declined.
